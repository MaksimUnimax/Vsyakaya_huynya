## 2. What the author actually discovered

The central observation is about **settlement arithmetic**, not forecasting BTC direction.

Kalshi crypto contracts use a 60-second arithmetic average of CF Benchmarks RTI observations at one-second intervals around expiration. A last-second BTC tick does not directly become the settlement; it is only one contribution to a 60-observation mean.

Therefore, as the settlement minute progresses, part of the final result becomes irreversible. Even before most samples are physically locked, a sufficiently large estimated margin can make the probability of a flip tiny after modeling the remaining seconds.

Vela uses Binance as a high-frequency proxy for the RTI, estimates/updates the Binance-to-RTI basis causally, reconstructs the settlement margin, estimates uncertainty in the unobserved remainder and converts it into `p_side` — probability that the chosen side will settle at $1.

This creates a research question:

> Does the market sometimes react to the **latest spot price** more strongly than the **60-second settlement average** deserves, temporarily selling a side whose settlement probability remains very high?

## 3. Initial historical evidence: lock detection

The repo's June 9 research documents 6,308 settled KXBTC15M windows covering 2026-04-03 -> 2026-06-09, plus 1-second Binance prices and a large Kalshi trade sample.

The reported/audited lock-detection results include:

- tau=30 seconds, |estimated settlement margin| > $50: 1 wrong side out of 3,848 = 99.974%;
- tau=30, margin > $75: 0 wrong out of 2,903;
- tau=45, margin > $50: synthesis reports 0 wrong out of 3,833;
- a separate red-team analysis in the repo says the causal de-bias, timestamp alignment and sample completeness on that historical dataset were sound and found no look-ahead leakage.

A genuine model miss is documented: one tau=30 / $50-margin case where the estimate favored NO by ~$51 but the final settlement finished about $4.49 on the YES side. This is useful evidence against the idea that the author simply hid errors.

## 4. First strategy failed: buy the obvious near-locked winner as a taker

Very high prediction accuracy did NOT create a tradeable edge.

When the model became confident, Kalshi was already pricing the favored side around 0.985-0.999. The remaining gross value was too small after execution price and fees. The repo's independent red-team replication reports taker net EV negative across the tested grid, roughly -0.55 to -1.10 cents per contract with realistic taker execution.

Key lesson:

`accurate forecast != profitable trade`

If market price already encodes your forecast, there is no edge left to buy.

## 5. Important self-correction: the repo initially modeled maker fees incorrectly

An early `maker_economics.md` analysis treated the maker fee as though the cent round-up occurred separately on every contract. That made high-price maker fills appear to cost roughly 1 cent per contract and pushed the analysis toward "maker is dead too."

The later canonical `backtest/strategy_search/fees.py` explicitly corrects this:

`maker fee = round_up_cent(0.0175 * C * P * (1-P))`

where `C` is the total number of contracts in the order and the cent round-up happens **once per order**, not once per contract.

The code's own example for a 100-contract order at 0.97:

- old wrong model: about $1.00 fee;
- corrected maker model: about $0.06 fee;
- around 16x difference.

This is a major methodological event in the project. Any early maker conclusions that depend on the per-contract 1c floor must be treated as superseded.

## 6. Second important failure: "every cheap print is panic" was wrong

A later strategy-search note documents an early live/paper result:

- 14 small wins;
- 1 large loss around -$5.357;
- total net around -$4.23.

The losing fill was around 0.28 with only about +$30 model margin. The program had interpreted a very cheap favored-side price as a wonderful bargain. The author later realized that a 28c market price is not a small panic discount — it is the market strongly saying the model may be wrong.

Backtest diagnosis at loose ~$10 margin reportedly found many losing windows / losing fills concentrated around very cheap ~0.18-0.20 prices; at much stronger margin gates (e.g. >=$40 in that analysis), those historical losses disappeared.

This led to a more nuanced rule:

- require strong model probability;
- require the market to still mostly agree that this side is favored (`win_px` above a floor);
- only fade a moderate panic discount from a high base;
- do NOT catch a side the entire market has repriced as a longshot.

The strategy-search code explicitly tests combined model-probability + market-agreement gates and checks that the bad 0.28 fill would have been rejected.

## 7. What "panic fade" means in plain language

Suppose the contract pays $1 if the final 60-second average ends above a threshold.

The model thinks the YES side has, say, ~98-99% chance after accounting for the already-observed average and remaining seconds.

The market may normally price YES around 97-99c.

Then BTC suddenly ticks down. Traders watching the latest spot panic and sell YES. But because the settlement is an average, that tick may not change the final average nearly as much as the crowd emotionally assumes.

Vela does NOT want YES at any price. It tries to place/rest a cheaper maker bid and waits for a panic seller to hit it. The intended edge is therefore:

`settlement smoothing + transient last-price overreaction + passive execution`

not:

`predict BTC direction better than everyone else`.

## 9. Public live fill calibration

Current repo commits a derived `analysis/data/fill_calibration_btc.parquet` and the producing script.

Committed report:

- 1,034 distinct maker-fade attempts;
- 88 filled;
- fill rate 8.5%;
- model `p_side` on filled trades: 0.9795;
- realized win rate on filled trades: 0.9659 (n=88);
- mean model `p_side` on unfilled attempts: 0.9976 (n=946);
- reported mean PnL per filled attempt: +$0.1803;
- mean filled size: 7 contracts.

This is an important reality check. The apparent opportunities with the highest modeled probability tend NOT to fill. Trades that actually reach the resting bid are somewhat worse. That is classic fill-conditional adverse selection.

A separate committed coverage report says all 1,356 analyzed placements had a same-ticker order-book snapshot within 2 seconds, 1,354 within 1 second. That makes the execution research substantially more useful than a price-only backtest.

## 12. Current September-2026 rule check

Kalshi's current crypto help material still states that crypto expiration values are calculated by averaging 60 CF Benchmarks RTI observations collected once per second during the expiration minute. It also states RTI data is available through the Kalshi API as a direct pass-through.

Therefore the settlement mechanism underlying Vela still exists as of 2026-09-17.

That does **not** prove the June-2026 pricing anomaly remains. The market can learn even if contract rules remain unchanged.

## 13. What we can independently test

### A. Reproduce the arithmetic

Collect fresh RTI + BTC/ETH proxy data and calculate the final-60s settlement estimate each second. Measure how often a side that appears near-locked actually flips.

This directly tests the structural premise without placing any orders.

### B. Reproduce the market mismatch

Simultaneously record Kalshi order book/trades. For every near-locked state ask:

- what is model probability?
- what does Kalshi imply?
- does the favored side temporarily sell off after a late spot shock?
- how deep and how long is the discount?

This tells us whether the mispricing still exists now.

### C. Model actual fillability

A historical trade at 90c does not prove our 90c resting order would have filled. Record queue/depth and simulate our position in queue. Measure:

- fill rate;
- time to fill;
- partial fill rate;
- price/depth ahead;
- conditional model error after fill.

### D. Freeze and forward-test

Choose gates BEFORE the evaluation period. Do not tune them after seeing outcomes.

Track:

- attempts;
- fills;
- W/L;
- realized/paper PnL after exact fees;
- average win;
- worst loss;
- CVaR/tail loss;
- calibration of p_side;
- fill-vs-unfilled adverse selection;
- result by BTC/ETH and market series.

### E. Only then consider tiny live reconciliation

If the paper/queue-aware test survives, a later tiny-capital experiment can reconcile exchange-confirmed fills/fees/settlements. Do not infer live edge from backtest ROI alone.

## 14. How we can USE the finding without blindly copying the bot

The reusable research pattern is broader than Kalshi BTC:

1. Read settlement rules literally, not the market title.
2. Look for contracts where the final result is a function of many samples / an average / a bounded calculation.
3. Determine whether part of the result becomes mathematically fixed before trading stops.
4. Identify what visible headline variable humans are likely reacting to instead (last price, latest score, latest poll, latest weather reading).
5. Compute a causal fair probability from the actual settlement function.
6. Look for temporary disagreement between this probability and the order book.
7. Measure liquidity, queue and adverse selection before treating it as executable.

Potential search domains:

- averaged crypto/index settlements;
- weather contracts settled from a multi-reading daily statistic;
- sports qualification/placement contracts where combinatorial constraints lock outcomes before the crowd reprices every derivative market;
- ladder/range contracts with arithmetic consistency constraints;
- poll/election aggregate contracts with predetermined data-source windows;
- any event contract where resolution uses a lagged/averaged formula while participants focus on the freshest headline observation.

The valuable idea is **settlement-function arbitrage research**, not a promise that Vela's exact June trade is still profitable.
